1. “Unseen Cosmos” - Sir Francis Graham Smith

Disclaimer: No AI was used to write this review.

Sir Francis Graham Smith is now a retired radio astronomer from UK and has been one of the pioneers in the field of radio astronomy. He started his early career with Sir Martin Ryle who introduced interferrometry to radio astronomy. Francis also was honored with title of “Astronomer Royal(1982-1990)”. In this book he basically provides us with an account of how we started to know about the world outside of solar system, our current understanding of universe based on the cosmolgical observations and exciting discoveries that awaits us in future that will also help us in expand our horizon of understanding regarding unseen cosmos. Himself being a radio astronomer, this book provides our evolutionary understanding of cosmos based on evidences mostly from radio astronomical observations. In this review, I provide brief account of each chapter in the book.

Chapter One – Radio Noise from Space

Starts with how observational astronomy started its journey with Galileo first publishing the observation of sky in 1610 with his newly invented telescope. This was a beginning of human scientific endeavor of understanding universe through observation of heaven. Radio astronomical observation started 340 years later with its beginning marked by the discovery of radio noise from outer space while Karl Jansky was from Bell Labs was trying to investigate long-distance radio communication. With his steerable antenna Jansky could detect the radio noise in the sky. This radio noise was identified to be coming outer space and strongest signal came from Milky Way, especially from the constellation of Sagittarius, which contains the center of our Galaxy.

Little notice was made about this first human detection of radio noise by astronomical community though the news made its place in New York Times while official technical work was published in proceedings of the Institute of Radio Engineers – journal less known to astronomers. Grote Reber, another radio engineer and a hobbyist, built a first radio telescope in his backyard after learning about Karl Jansky’s work. With his backyard reflector radio telescope 9.5m in diameter, Reber was able to draw maps of radio emission from Milky Way using 160MHz frequency(187cm wavelength). He had no astronomical training and went to take course in University of Chicago where he eventually convinced Otto Sturve, director of Yerkes observatory to see his results and also managed to publish those results in Astrophysical Journal. Reber kept on following his work with improved receivers and suceeded in getting radio maps of milky way with finer details.

Both the telescope used by Karl Jansky and Grote Reber, exists for demonstration at Green Bank, West Viriginia – a radio telescope facility of National Radio Astronomical Observatory(NRAO). On the other side of Atlantic, radio astronomy started to catch up with radars used during the war that were no more actively used after the end of second world war. Bernard Lovell, a pioneer in radar during world war II, built radio telescope to study cosmic ray showers.These pioneering works in early days of radio astronomy was followed by active research interests and need to conduct space research after 1950s that led to the development of larger reflector radio telescopes such as Lovell Telescope at Jordell Bank in Manchester.

Chapter Two – Hot Sun and Cold Planets

Sun as we see it, is not a complete picture. What we see is optical wavelength picture of sun. Though sun is mostly quiet in radio it still emits radio emission at some wavelength. Based on its radio emission properties we can infer the temperature of the sun. First radio measurements of the temperature of the quiet sun were made by George Southworth of Bell Labs. Southworth measured a temperature of 10,000K somewhat higher than the 6000K temperature of the visible sun. Joseph Pawsey from Australia measured temperature of 1million K at a wavelength of 1.5m. These radio observations led to the realization that radio and light(optical) were not coming from the same part of the visible surface.

This chapter also discusses how the radio interferrometer techinque was developed and used for measuring sunspots. Radio interferometer was first developed by Martin Ryle in Cambridge using theoretical idea from already used optical intereferrometer first developed by Michelson Morley and used to measure the angular size of Betelgeuse star. An interesting story of serendipitous discovery of solar flare during world war II while trying to detect enemy battleships using radars informs us on how effort on different mission can sometime lead to the interesting scientific discovery. It also presents how radio interferrometric techniques can be used to understand the surfaces of other planets in the solar system along with meteor which some time emits radio wave due to its burning tail as it passes through the atmosphere with a very high speed. The advantage of radio over optical in studying planets or sun is that radio waves can penetrate the outer gaseous surface and help us to understand the few centimeters below the surface visible in optical.

Chapter Three – Our Galaxy: the Milky Way

In this chapter, author digs into radio astronomer’s early effort into knowing the structure of galaxy through radio telescopes. He starts with account of an early effort by Jan Oort to map the HI content of the Milky Way galaxy with the help of his student Van de Hulst, both being Dutch astronomers. They started the 21cm neutral hydrogen(HI) line revolution in radio astronomy which has been a major breakthrough in our attempt to understanding the galaxies as much of matter content in the universe is HI. This chapter also touches on the latter efforts to understand the magnetic field distribution in the Milky Way galaxy by measuring the Faraday rotation caused due to dispersion at different radio frequency measurements.

Chapter Four – Cosmic Rays, the Synchrotron, and Molecules

After the war ended, radars used during the war were turned into a scientific research device. Smith, in the first part of this chapter, details about how Lovell telescope in Jodrell Bank, Manchester came into existence as a mission to understand the cosmic ray shower led by Sir Bernard Lovell and Patrick Blackett who had joint interest in pursuing research into the origin and track of cosmic ray shower.

As charged particles(electrons, protons) gyrate around the magnetic lines of force, they emit radiation which we call synchrotron radiation. It was later realized that comic ray particles should be emitting radiation as they pass through galactic magnetic field and since the curvature of the galactic magnetic field lines are very high the radiation emitted must be in low radio frequency part of the part of the electromagnetic spectrum. This understanding led to connection between synchrotron radiation and cosmic ray showers.

This chapter ends with discussion of presence of large variety of molecules found in galactic clouds. Lot of these molecules are detectable at millimeter wavelengths using radio telescopes that operate at cm to mm wavelengths. Study of these molecules help us to understand various problems in astrophysics such as how stars are born by clumping of these molecular clouds of gas and dust.

Chapter Five – Radio Galaxies and Quasars

James Hey, in 1946, while mapping the radio emission from the Milky Way, noticed a remarkable fluctuating radio signal from some unknown location in constellation of Cygnus and the spectra of this source looked like that of stars. Later, John Bolton in Australia and Martin Ryle in Cambridge UK, used interferrometer to study these radio emitting sources that had spectra similar to that of stars. More of such sources were discovered and later collaboration with optical astronomers, mostly at Mount Wilson and Mount Palomar, to get visible image of these resources revealed that these sources were much farther away beyond our own Milky Way galaxy. These were the first discovery of astrophysical objects outside of Milky Way and named quasars for their quasi stellar spectra. Author presents a historical account of study of radio galaxies and how black holes were learnt to be residing as a central engine. This interesting discovery enlightened astronomers about the potential of radio astronomy to probe universe at cosmological scale.

Chapter Six – Supernovae and Pulsars

“Cosmic rays are caused by exploding stars which burn with a fire equal to 100 million suns and then shrivel from 0.5 million miles diameter to little spheres 14 miles thick”. F. Smith, with this legendary quote by Fritz Zwicky, opens up this chapter and explains how this prediction made by Zwicky turned out to be true along with the discovery of the first pulsar by Antony Hewish and Jocelyn Bell. Author himself being one of the pioneers in the field of pulsar astronomy, initially gives a brief historical account of initial pulsar discovery days and later explains in detail about the physical mechanism behind radiation emitted in various frequencies from radio to gamma.

Chapter Seven – Pulsar clocks and Relativity

This chapter basically reviews how study of pulsar can be used for tests of gravity. Binary pulsars orbiting each other was first used as the indirect verification for the existence of gravitational radiation by Joe Taylor and Russel Hulse. As two binary pulsar orbit each other they lose energy in the form of gravitational radiation and the orbits shrink eventually leading to coalescence of these two pulsars. It has also been explained about the next big project in astronomy about the detection of low frequency gravitational waves that will be detected by measuring the changes in pulse arrival time from several standard millisecond pulsars. This change is pulse arrival time happens as gravitational wave pass by from events such as merger of super-massive black holes at the center of galaxies.

Chapter Eight – Radio expands into cosmology

Before Hubble experimentally showed that nearby galaxies were moving away from each other, universe was believed to be steady. With the 100 inch optical telescope, Hubble could only observe to distances with redshift(z) 0.01. In order to make any firm conclusion whether universe was actually expanding, it was necessary to observe galaxies to higher red shifts. This necessity led radio astronomers to jump into the cosmological study of universe by observing radio galaxies at higher redshifts. With spectra of hundreds of radio galaxies observed and analysed it was conclusively proved that universe was actually expanding. Later studies, proved that universe was not only expanding but this expansion was also accelerating. Several astronomical observations prove that universe is in a state of an accelerated expansion.

Chapter Nine – Seeing the Cosmic Fireball

In chapter eight, observational evidence that supports the accelerated expansion of the universe has been made. In chapter nine, author sheds light on how did the universe began especially 370,000 years after a big bang, a timeline only up to which we can look back into. Several spacecraft(COBE, WMAP, Planck) equipped with telescopes to probe at millimeter wavelengths were launched to study the cosmic microwave background. Optical light emitted at this timeline in the evolutionary history of the universe would be redshifted to wavelengths of ~milimeters as the universe expanded. Analysis of data from these mission supports that fluctuations in the early universe led to clumping of structure which eventually evolved to form large scale structures like galaxies and cluster of galaxies. It also helped to firmly establish that the universe consisted of ~5% baryonic matter that are observable and make up galaxies, stars, gas and dust. 27% of the universe constitutes of dark matter and ~68% is dark energy. Chapter Ten – Big Dishes and Arrays Big dishes are important because of their higher sensitivity where as to make detailed maps or radio sources and locate them, radio interferometers play an imminent role. Author himself being involved in an initial development of radio interferometer with the legendary Martin Ryle, provides a detailed account of the historical development of radio telescopes in this chapter.

Chapter Eleven – LOFAR, ALMA and SKA

F G Smith, in this final chapter, outlines prospective future telescopes low frequency array(LOFAR), Atacama large milimeter array(ALMA) and square kilometer array(SKA) and the unique regime of radio frequencies they will be looking at with their own strengths. All of these are either partially or fully operational and have made important discoveries. Once all these radio telescopes come into full fledge operation, there is no doubt many important discoveries awaits us expanding our understanding of unseen cosmos.
